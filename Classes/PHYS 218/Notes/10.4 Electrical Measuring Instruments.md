# 10.4 Electrical Measuring Instruments
Class: [[PHYS 218]] · Textbook: UPV2 §10.4 · Related: [[Exam 2 Review - Ch 8-10]] · [[Physics Intuition - Ch 5-10]] · [[Flashcards - PHYS 218 Circuits (Ch 9-10)]]

> [!abstract] Big picture
> A meter has to measure a circuit **without changing it**. That one requirement decides everything: an **ammeter** goes **in series** and needs a **tiny** resistance, a **voltmeter** goes **in parallel** and needs a **huge** resistance, and an **ohmmeter** only works on a component that's **disconnected** from everything else.

---

## Ammeters measure current: in series, near-zero resistance

To measure the current *through* something, the current has to pass *through the meter*. So you break the circuit and put the ammeter **in series** with that component. Things in series carry the same current, so the meter reads the component's current.

```tikz
\usepackage{circuitikz}
\begin{document}
\begin{circuitikz}
\draw (0,3) to[battery1, l_=$\varepsilon$] (0,0);
\draw (0,3) -- (1.12,3); \node[draw, circle, minimum size=0.75cm, inner sep=0pt] at (1.5,3) {A}; \draw (1.88,3) -- (3,3) to[R, l=$R_1$] (3,1.5) to[R, l=$R_2$] (3,0) -- (0,0);
\node at (1.5,-0.6) {(a) series: one ammeter};
\end{circuitikz}
\end{document}
```

```tikz
\usepackage{circuitikz}
\begin{document}
\begin{circuitikz}
\draw (0,3) to[battery1, l_=$\varepsilon$] (0,0);
\draw (0,3) -- (0.87,3); \node[draw, circle, minimum size=0.75cm, inner sep=0pt] at (1.25,3) {A}; \draw (1.63,3) -- (5.5,3);
\draw (2.5,3) node[circ]{} -- (2.5,2.55);
\node[draw, circle, minimum size=0.75cm, inner sep=0pt] at (2.5,2.17) {A$_1$};
\draw (2.5,1.8) to[R, l_=$R_1$] (2.5,0) node[circ]{};
\draw (5.5,3) -- (5.5,2.55);
\node[draw, circle, minimum size=0.75cm, inner sep=0pt] at (5.5,2.17) {A$_2$};
\draw (5.5,1.8) to[R, l=$R_2$] (5.5,0) -- (0,0);
\node at (2.75,-0.6) {(b) parallel: one per branch};
\end{circuitikz}
\end{document}
```

- **(a) Series:** one ammeter anywhere in the loop reads the current through everything, since it's all the same current.
- **(b) Parallel:** each branch has its own current, so you need a meter **in each branch** ($A_1$, $A_2$), plus one on the main line for the battery's total ($A = A_1 + A_2$, by the junction rule).

**Why the resistance must be tiny.** The ammeter becomes part of the circuit, so its resistance $R_A$ adds in series:
$$I_{measured} = \frac{\varepsilon}{R_{circuit} + R_A} \quad\text{instead of}\quad \frac{\varepsilon}{R_{circuit}}$$
That's only accurate when $R_A \ll R_{circuit}$. Real ammeters are a fraction of a milliohm. They also contain a **fuse**, because all of the circuit's current goes through them.

> [!example] Example: a bad ammeter
> A 1.5 V battery (ignore its $r$) drives a 2.0 Ω resistor. The true current is $1.5/2.0 = 0.75$ A.
> - With a 0.5 Ω ammeter: $I = 1.5/(2.0 + 0.5) =$ **0.60 A**. That's 20% low.
> - With a 1 mΩ ammeter: $I = 1.5/2.001 =$ **0.7496 A**. Off by about 0.05%, which is fine.

## Voltmeters measure voltage: in parallel, huge resistance

Voltage is a difference *across* something, between two points. So the voltmeter's two leads go on either side of the component, putting it **in parallel**. Things in parallel have the same voltage, so the meter reads the component's voltage. You don't have to break the circuit.

```tikz
\usepackage{circuitikz}
\begin{document}
\begin{circuitikz}
\draw (0,3) to[battery1, l_=$\varepsilon$] (0,1.6) to[R, l_=$r$] (0,0);
\draw[dashed, gray] (-0.95,3.3) rectangle (0.45,-0.3);
\node[gray] at (-0.25,-0.6) {battery};
\draw (0,3) -- (3,3) to[R, l_=$R_1$] (3,1.5) to[R, l_=$R_2$, *-*] (3,0) -- (0,0);
\draw (3,1.5) -- (4.6,1.5) -- (4.6,1.13);
\node[draw, circle, minimum size=0.75cm, inner sep=0pt] at (4.6,0.75) {V};
\draw (4.6,0.37) -- (4.6,0) -- (3,0);
\end{circuitikz}
\end{document}
```

*The voltmeter here reads $V_2$, the voltage across $R_2$.*

**Why the resistance must be huge.** The voltmeter is an extra branch in parallel. Any current it pulls lowers the resistance of the piece it's measuring, which changes that piece's voltage. That's called **loading**. It only disappears when $R_V \gg R$. Cheap digital meters are around 10 MΩ, and precision meters are higher.

> [!example] Example: loading a voltage divider
> A 12 V battery is connected to two 100 kΩ resistors in series. The true voltage across each one is **6.0 V**.
> - **Old analog meter, $R_V = 200$ kΩ:** in parallel with 100 kΩ it makes $\frac{(100)(200)}{300} = 66.7$ kΩ. The divider becomes 100 kΩ and 66.7 kΩ, so the meter reads $12 \times \frac{66.7}{166.7} =$ **4.8 V**. That's 20% low.
> - **Digital meter, $R_V = 10$ MΩ:** $100\text{ k} \parallel 10\text{ M} = 99.0$ kΩ, so it reads $12 \times \frac{99.0}{199.0} =$ **5.97 V**. Close.
>
> Loading only matters when the resistor you're measuring is big. Across a 100 Ω resistor, even the 200 kΩ meter would be nearly perfect.

**You can't measure an emf directly.** A battery's internal resistance $r$ is inside the battery, so your leads can only touch its terminals. A voltmeter there reads the **terminal voltage** $V = \varepsilon - Ir$. It reads $\approx \varepsilon$ only when **no current is flowing**: the battery is disconnected from everything except the voltmeter, which draws almost nothing.

## Inside an analog meter: the galvanometer

Analog meters are built around a **galvanometer**: a small coil in a magnetic field with a needle attached (the magnetism is Ch 11). Current through the coil makes it twist, and the needle hits full scale at a small current $I_G$, typically µA to mA. The coil has a resistance $R_G$. On its own it's a very sensitive, very fragile ammeter. You turn it into a useful meter with one extra resistor.

```tikz
\usepackage{circuitikz}
\begin{document}
\begin{circuitikz}
\draw (0,1.5) node[circ]{} -- (1.12,1.5); \node[draw, circle, minimum size=0.75cm, inner sep=0pt] at (1.5,1.5) {G}; \node at (1.5,2.15) {$R_G$}; \draw (1.88,1.5) -- (3,1.5) node[circ]{};
\draw (0,1.5) |- (0.5,0) to[R, l_=$R_{sh}$] (2.5,0) -| (3,1.5);
\draw (-0.8,1.5) to[short, o-] (0,1.5); \draw (3,1.5) to[short, -o] (3.8,1.5);
\node at (1.5,-1) {(a) ammeter: small shunt in parallel};
\end{circuitikz}
\end{document}
```

```tikz
\usepackage{circuitikz}
\begin{document}
\begin{circuitikz}
\draw (0,1.5) to[short, o-] (1.02,1.5); \node[draw, circle, minimum size=0.75cm, inner sep=0pt] at (1.4,1.5) {G}; \node at (1.4,2.15) {$R_G$}; \draw (1.78,1.5) -- (2.4,1.5) to[R, l=$R$] (4.4,1.5) to[short, -o] (4.8,1.5);
\node at (2.4,-1) {(b) voltmeter: big resistor in series};
\end{circuitikz}
\end{document}
```

> [!example] Derivation: ammeter shunt
> To read currents up to $I$, put a small **shunt** $R_{sh}$ **in parallel** with the galvanometer so most of the current bypasses the coil. At full scale the coil carries $I_G$, the shunt carries the rest, $I - I_G$, and both have the same voltage across them (they're in parallel):
> $$I_G R_G = (I - I_G)R_{sh} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{R_{sh} = \frac{I_G R_G}{I - I_G}}$$
> The whole meter is $R_G \parallel R_{sh}$, which is even smaller than $R_{sh}$. That's exactly what an ammeter needs.

> [!example] Derivation: voltmeter series resistor
> To read voltages up to $V$, put a big resistor $R$ **in series** with the galvanometer so that $V$ across the whole thing drives exactly the full-scale current:
> $$V = I_G(R_G + R) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{R = \frac{V}{I_G} - R_G}$$
> The whole meter is $R_G + R$, which is huge. That's exactly what a voltmeter needs.

> [!example] Example: one galvanometer, two meters
> A galvanometer has $R_G = 25\ \Omega$ and reads full scale at $I_G = 50\ \mu$A.
> - **0–1 A ammeter:** $R_{sh} = \dfrac{(50\times10^{-6})(25)}{1 - 50\times10^{-6}} \approx$ **1.25 mΩ** in parallel. The total meter resistance is $\approx 1.25$ mΩ.
> - **0–10 V voltmeter:** $R = \dfrac{10}{50\times10^{-6}} - 25 =$ **199 975 Ω** in series. The total meter resistance is 200 kΩ.

**Digital meters** skip the galvanometer. An analog-to-digital converter turns the voltage into a number for the display. They can detect much smaller currents than a galvanometer, so a digital voltmeter can have a much higher resistance (it barely needs any current to read). That's why they disturb the circuit less and read more accurately.

> [!question]- Check Your Understanding 10.8: Digital meters detect smaller currents than galvanometer meters. Why does that make them more accurate?
> A meter that needs less current to register can be built to **draw** less current. A digital voltmeter can have a far higher resistance, so its branch steals almost no current and barely changes the voltage it's measuring (less loading). For a digital ammeter, needing only a tiny voltage drop to read means its resistance can be even smaller. Either way, the meter disturbs the circuit less, so the reading is closer to the true value.

## Ohmmeters measure resistance: disconnected components only

An ohmmeter is Ohm's law in a box: $R = V/I$.
- **Old style:** an internal battery pushes current through the component, a galvanometer measures the current, and $R$ is calculated from it.
- **Digital:** a constant-current source pushes a known current through the component, and the meter measures the voltage across it.

Two rules:
1. **Isolate the component.** If it's still wired into a circuit, the ohmmeter's current also flows through every other path, and you measure the **equivalent resistance** of the whole network. For example, a 100 Ω resistor still connected in parallel with a 300 Ω one reads $\frac{(100)(300)}{400} =$ **75 Ω**.
2. **Never on a live circuit.** An external voltage source adds its own current and voltage, which gives a wrong reading and can damage the meter.

---

## Cheat sheet

| Meter | Connect it… | Its resistance should be… | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ammeter | in **series** (break the circuit) | **very small** ($\ll R$ of the circuit) | so it doesn't reduce the current it measures |
| Voltmeter | in **parallel** (across the part) | **very large** ($\gg R$ of the part) | so it doesn't steal current and lower the voltage |
| Ohmmeter | across a **disconnected** part | (has its own source) | otherwise it reads the whole network, or gets damaged |

| Build from a galvanometer ($R_G$, $I_G$) | Formula |
|---|---|
| Ammeter, range $I$: shunt **in parallel** | $R_{sh} = \dfrac{I_G R_G}{I - I_G}$ |
| Voltmeter, range $V$: resistor **in series** | $R = \dfrac{V}{I_G} - R_G$ |

> [!warning] Common mistakes
> - **Ammeter in parallel:** its near-zero resistance **shorts** the component. A huge current flows through the meter and blows its fuse.
> - **Voltmeter in series:** its huge resistance acts like a **break**. The current drops to almost zero, and the meter reads roughly the full source voltage. (It doesn't harm anything, but the reading means nothing.)
> - **Mixing up the two designs:** the *ammeter* gets the *parallel shunt* (small), and the *voltmeter* gets the *series resistor* (big).
> - **"The voltmeter reads $\varepsilon$":** only with no current flowing. Under load it reads $\varepsilon - Ir$.
> - **Ohmmeter on a part still in the circuit:** you get the equivalent resistance, not the part's own.

## Practice

**1.** You want the current through $R_2$ and the voltage across $R_1$ in a circuit where $R_1$ and $R_2$ are in parallel across a battery. Where does each meter go?
- 

**2.** A galvanometer has $R_G = 10\ \Omega$ and full-scale current 1.0 mA. Design (a) a 0–5 A ammeter and (b) a 0–20 V voltmeter. Say series or parallel for each.
- 

**3.** A battery with $\varepsilon = 9.0$ V and $r = 1.0\ \Omega$ is checked with a 1.0 MΩ voltmeter, first by itself, then while it's also connected to an 8.0 Ω load. What does the meter read each time?
- 

**4.** Two 10 kΩ resistors in series are across 10 V. What does a voltmeter with 10 kΩ of internal resistance read across one of them? What about a 1.0 MΩ voltmeter?
- 

**5.** Someone puts an ammeter in series with a 4.0 Ω resistor across an ideal 12 V battery. The ammeter's resistance is 0.20 Ω. What does it read, and what's the percent error?
- 

> [!success]- Answers
> **1.** Put an ammeter **in series in the $R_2$ branch** (between the junction and $R_2$). Put the voltmeter **in parallel across $R_1$**. Since $R_1$ and $R_2$ are in parallel with the battery, it reads the same as across $R_2$ or across the battery's terminals.
>
> **2.** (a) Shunt **in parallel**: $R_{sh} = \dfrac{(1.0\times10^{-3})(10)}{5 - 0.001} \approx$ **2.0 mΩ**. (b) Resistor **in series**: $R = \dfrac{20}{1.0\times10^{-3}} - 10 =$ **19 990 Ω**.
>
> **3.** Alone: $I = 9.0/(1\,000\,000 + 1) \approx 9\ \mu$A, so $V = \varepsilon - Ir \approx$ **9.0 V** (it reads the emf because almost no current flows). With the 8.0 Ω load, the meter's own current is negligible: $I = 9.0/(8.0 + 1.0) = 1.0$ A, so $V = 9.0 - 1.0(1.0) =$ **8.0 V**.
>
> **4.** True value: 5.0 V. A 10 kΩ meter makes the measured piece $10\,\text{k} \parallel 10\,\text{k} = 5$ kΩ, so it reads $10 \times \frac{5}{15} \approx$ **3.3 V**. A 1.0 MΩ meter: $10\,\text{k} \parallel 1\,\text{M} \approx 9.90$ kΩ, so it reads $\approx$ **4.98 V**.
>
> **5.** $I = 12/(4.0 + 0.20) \approx$ **2.86 A** instead of $12/4.0 = 3.00$ A, which is about **4.8% low**. The ammeter's resistance isn't small enough compared with 4 Ω.

## What you should know
> [!success] By the end of this section you should be able to…
> 1. **Place meters correctly:** an ammeter in series (one per branch you care about), a voltmeter in parallel across the part.
> 2. **Explain the resistances:** ammeters near zero, voltmeters huge, both so the meter doesn't change what it measures.
> 3. **Calculate the error** a real meter introduces (ammeter: adds series $R$; voltmeter: adds a parallel branch).
> 4. **Design meters from a galvanometer** with $R_{sh} = \frac{I_GR_G}{I - I_G}$ and $R = \frac{V}{I_G} - R_G$.
> 5. **Use an ohmmeter correctly:** isolate the part, and never on a live circuit.
> 6. **Know that a voltmeter on a battery reads the terminal voltage,** $\varepsilon - Ir$, and equals $\varepsilon$ only with no current.
