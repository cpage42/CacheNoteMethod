## 11.2 Force on a moving charge

$$\vec F = q\,\vec v\times\vec B \qquad F = qvB\sin\theta$$

- There's no force if the charge is at rest or $\vec v\parallel\vec B$. The force is largest when $\vec v\perp\vec B$.
- The force is ⟂ to $\vec v$, so it **does no work**. Speed stays constant and only the direction changes.
- **RHR-1:** fingers along $\vec v$, curl them toward $\vec B$, and your thumb gives $\vec F$ for a **+** charge. **Flip it for negative charges.**
- Unit vectors: $\hat x\times\hat y=\hat z$, $\hat y\times\hat z=\hat x$, $\hat z\times\hat x=\hat y$ (reversing the order flips the sign). ⊙ = out of the page, ⊗ = into the page.

## 11.3 Motion in a uniform field

| What | Equation |
|---|---|
| Radius | $r=\dfrac{mv}{qB}$ |
| Period | $T=\dfrac{2\pi m}{qB}$ (independent of $v$) |
| Helix ($\vec v$ not ⟂ $\vec B$) | $r=\dfrac{mv_\perp}{qB}$, pitch $p=v_\parallel T$ |

> [!tip] Strategy: circular-motion problems
> 1. The magnetic force is the centripetal force: $qvB = mv^2/r$.
> 2. Write down $m$ and $q$ for the particle **first** (proton, electron, alpha = $4.0015$ u and $+2e$), in **SI units**.
> 3. If it was accelerated through a voltage, get $v$ from $qV=\tfrac12mv^2$.
> 4. Sanity check: proton orbits are in mm–m, and cyclotron frequencies are in MHz.

## 11.4 Force on a current-carrying wire

$$\vec F = I\vec l\times\vec B \qquad F = IlB\sin\theta \qquad d\vec F = I\,d\vec l\times\vec B$$

- $I$ is the current **in the wire feeling the force**. The field comes from something else.
- **A closed loop in a uniform field feels zero net force.** In a non-uniform field (near a wire, for example), the sides don't balance.

> [!tip] Strategy: who makes the field, who feels it
> 1. **Source** (makes $B$): its current goes into the $B$ formula.
> 2. **Target** (feels $F$): its current and length go into $F=IlB$.
> 3. A loop's forces on itself always cancel (Newton's 3rd law). Only **external** fields matter.

## 11.5 Force and torque on a current loop

| What | Equation |
|---|---|
| Magnetic dipole moment | $\vec\mu = NI\vec A$, magnitude $\mu=NIA$ |
| Torque | $\vec\tau=\vec\mu\times\vec B$, $\ \tau=\mu B\sin\theta = NIAB\sin\theta$ |
| Potential energy | $U = -\vec\mu\cdot\vec B = -\mu B\cos\theta$ |

- **Direction of $\vec\mu$:** curl your fingers along the current, and your thumb gives $\vec\mu$, which is ⟂ to the loop.
- The torque turns $\vec\mu$ **toward** $\vec B$. The torque is largest when $\mu\perp B$ (with $U=0$) and zero when $\mu\parallel B$ (with $U=-\mu B$, which is the stable position).
- $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec\mu$ and $\vec B$, **not** between the loop's plane and $\vec B$.
- Circular coil: $\mu = NI\pi R^2$. Rectangle: $\mu=NIab$.

## 11.6 The Hall effect

| What | Equation |
|---|---|
| Drift speed | $v_d = \dfrac{I}{neA}$ |
| Balance (steady state) | $qE = qv_dB \Rightarrow E=v_dB$ |
| Hall voltage | $V = Bv_dl = \dfrac{IBl}{neA}$ |

- Here $l$ is the **width** the voltage is measured across, and $A$ is the cross-section.
- **Round wire** ($l=d$, $A=\pi d^2/4$): $V = \dfrac{4IB}{ne\pi d}$.
- Metals give **μV** (because $n$ is huge). Check what units the answer asks for.

## 11.7 Applications

| Device | Equation | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| Velocity selector | $v = \dfrac{E}{B}$ | $qE = qvB$: only one speed passes undeflected |
| Plates | $E = V/d$ | uniform field between parallel plates |
| Mass spectrometer | $\dfrac{m}{q}=\dfrac{BB_0r}{E}$ | selector fixes $v$, then $r=mv/qB_0$ |
| Cyclotron frequency | $f=\dfrac{qB}{2\pi m}$ | the AC must match the orbit frequency (no $v$) |
| Cyclotron max energy | $K=\dfrac{q^2B^2R^2}{2m}$ | particle exits at the dee radius $R$ |

## 12.1 Biot-Savart law (current → field)

$$d\vec B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{I\,d\vec l\times\hat r}{r^2} \qquad \vec B=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{I\,d\vec l\times\hat r}{r^2}$$

- $d\vec l$ is a small piece of wire **along the current**. $\vec r$ points **from that piece to the point P** where you want $B$.
- Each piece's field is ⟂ to both $d\vec l$ and $\hat r$. Pieces pointing straight at P contribute **nothing**.
- **RHR-2:** thumb along $I$, and your fingers curl the way $B$ circles the wire. (This is a different use of your hand from RHR-1.)

### Standard results (use these instead of integrating when the setup matches)

| Setup | Field at P |
|---|---|
| Infinite straight wire, distance $R$ | $B=\dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi R}$ |
| Finite straight wire, distance $R$ | $B=\dfrac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi R}(\sin\theta_2-\sin\theta_1)$ (angles measured from the perpendicular foot) |
| Circular arc of angle $\theta$ (**radians**), at its center | $B=\dfrac{\mu_0 I\theta}{4\pi R}$ |
| Full loop, at its center | $B=\dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2R}$ |
| Straight wire pointing at P (P on the wire's line) | $B=0$ |

> [!tip] Strategy: Biot-Savart, step by step
> 1. **Draw it.** Mark the point P, pick one small piece $d\vec l$ somewhere on the wire, and draw $\vec r$ from that piece **to P**.
> 2. **Direction first.** Use $d\vec l\times\hat r$ (or RHR-2). If every piece gives the **same direction** at P (true for arcs at their center and straight wires), you only need magnitudes.
> 3. **Use symmetry.** For pairs of pieces placed symmetrically, components often cancel. Keep only what survives.
> 4. **Magnitude of one piece:** $dB=\dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\dfrac{I\,dl\sin\phi}{r^2}$, where $\phi$ is the angle between $d\vec l$ and $\vec r$.
> 5. **Write $dl$, $r$ and $\phi$ in terms of one variable** (an angle or a position), then integrate. For an arc: $r=R$, $\phi=90^\circ$, $dl=R\,d\theta$, which gives $\mu_0I\theta/4\pi R$.
> 6. **Most of the time you can skip steps 4–5.** Break the wire into straight pieces and arcs, use the table for each, and **add as vectors** (pieces that point at P give 0).

> [!tip] Strategy: field from several wires (superposition)
> 1. For each wire: magnitude $B=\mu_0I/2\pi r$ at P.
> 2. Direction: RHR-2. At P, $B$ is ⟂ to the line from the wire to P.
> 3. Break each $B$ into components: $B_x=B\cos\theta$, $B_y=B\sin\theta$ (with the angle counterclockwise from $+x$).
> 4. Add the x's and the y's, then $|B|=\sqrt{B_x^2+B_y^2}$ and $\theta=\tan^{-1}(B_y/B_x)$, adding 180° if $B_x<0$.
> 5. If every field is ⟂ to the page (wires in the plane of the page), just add them as **± numbers** (out = +, into = −).

## Forces between wires (preview of 12.3, used in HW 11.4)

$$\frac{F}{l}=\frac{\mu_0 I_1I_2}{2\pi r}$$
Same-direction currents **attract**. Opposite currents **repel**. There's no $\sin\theta$ for parallel wires, which are always at 90°.

## Common mistakes

- Electron mass instead of proton mass, or forgetting that an alpha is $+2e$.
- Not converting cm/mm → m, or answering in V when it asks for μV.
- Mixing up RHR-1 (force) and RHR-2 (field).
- Adding vector forces or fields as plain numbers. Use components.
- Forgetting the bottom side of a loop near a wire. Both sides count, and $B$ depends on distance.
- Using degrees in the arc formula. It needs **radians**.
