# HW 10.4: Electrical measuring instruments

## The two ideas behind every meter problem
1. **An ammeter goes in series**, so it adds its own resistance to the circuit. A good one has tiny resistance, so it barely changes the current it's measuring.
2. **A voltmeter goes in parallel**, so it gives current a second path. A good one has huge resistance, so it barely steals any current.

Both meters are built from a **galvanometer**: a coil with resistance $r$ that reads full scale at a small current $I_G$ (its "sensitivity"). Every problem below is "how much does a real meter disturb the circuit" or "how do you build one."

## Problem 1: Building an ammeter from a galvanometer
> A 29 Ω galvanometer has 390 μA sensitivity. What resistance in parallel makes it an ammeter with a 10.0 A full-scale reading?

**What's happening.** The galvanometer maxes out at 390 μA, but we want to measure up to 10 A. So almost all of the 10 A has to **bypass** the galvanometer through a small **shunt resistor** in parallel. Only 390 μA goes through the coil when the total is 10 A.

```tikz
\usepackage{circuitikz}
\begin{document}
\begin{circuitikz}
\draw (0,0) -- (1,0) to[short,*-] (1,1.2) -- (2,1.2) node[circle,draw,minimum size=0.7cm](G){G} (G.east) -- (3,1.2) to[R,l=$r$] (5,1.2) -- (6,1.2) to[short,-*] (6,0) -- (7,0);
\draw (1,0) -- (1,-1.2) to[R,l_=$R_{sh}$] (6,-1.2) -- (6,0);
\draw (0,0) node[left]{$I$};
\end{circuitikz}
\end{document}
```

**Why this formula.** The galvanometer branch and the shunt are **in parallel**, and parallel branches always have the **same voltage** across them. (That's the loop rule: walk down one branch and back up the other and you must get zero.) So
$$\underbrace{I_G\,r}_{\text{voltage across galvanometer}} = \underbrace{(I - I_G)\,R_{sh}}_{\text{voltage across shunt}}$$
The shunt carries whatever the galvanometer doesn't: $I - I_G$ (junction rule).

**Solve.**
$$R_{sh} = \frac{I_G\,r}{I - I_G} = \frac{(390\times10^{-6}\ \text{A})(29\ \Omega)}{10.0\ \text{A} - 390\times10^{-6}\ \text{A}} = \frac{0.01131\ \text{V}}{9.99961\ \text{A}}$$
$$\boxed{R_{sh} = 1.131\times10^{-3}\ \Omega}$$

**Sanity check.** The shunt has to carry about 25,000 times more current than the galvanometer ($10/0.00039$), so its resistance should be about 25,000 times smaller than 29 Ω: $29/25{,}600 \approx 0.0011\ \Omega$ ✓. Since $I_G \ll I$, the shortcut $R_{sh} \approx I_G r/I$ works fine here.

## Problem 2: Measuring a battery with a real voltmeter
> A 3.200 V lithium cell with internal resistance $r = 35\ \Omega$ is measured by a $0.75$ kΩ voltmeter across its terminals. Find (a) the current, (b) the terminal voltage, (c) the ratio $V/\mathcal{E}$.

**What's happening.** A real battery = ideal emf $\mathcal{E}$ **in series with** its internal resistance $r$. Hooking up the voltmeter closes the loop, so current flows, and some voltage is lost inside the battery. The voltmeter reads only what's left at the terminals. That's why the problem says the voltmeter ends up "in series" (with $r$).

```tikz
\usepackage{circuitikz}
\begin{document}
\begin{circuitikz}
\draw (0,3) to[battery1,l_=$\mathcal{E}$] (0,1.5) to[R,l_=$r$] (0,0) -- (3,0);
\draw (0,3) -- (3,3);
\draw (3,3) -- (3,2) node[circle,draw,minimum size=0.7cm,anchor=north](V){V}; \draw (V.south) -- (3,0);
\draw[dashed] (-0.9,-0.3) rectangle (0.7,3.3); \node at (-0.1,-0.6) {\small battery};
\end{circuitikz}
\end{document}
```

**(a) Current.** One loop, so one current. The loop rule (or "series resistances add") gives
$$\mathcal{E} - Ir - IR = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{r + R} = \frac{3.200\ \text{V}}{35\ \Omega + 750\ \Omega} = \frac{3.200}{785}$$
$$\boxed{I = 4.076\times10^{-3}\ \text{A}}$$
(You entered 0.004100, which Expert TA accepted, but the exact value is 0.004076. Watch the rounding.)

**(b) Terminal voltage.** The voltmeter reads the voltage across its own resistance, which is also $\mathcal{E}$ minus the internal drop:
$$V = IR = (4.076\times10^{-3})(750) = \boxed{3.057\ \text{V}} \qquad\text{check: } \mathcal{E} - Ir = 3.200 - (4.076\times10^{-3})(35) = 3.057\ \text{V}\ ✓$$

**(c) Ratio.** Notice $\mathcal{E}$ cancels, so this is really a voltage divider:
$$\frac{V}{\mathcal{E}} = \frac{IR}{I(r + R)} = \frac{R}{r + R} = \frac{750}{785} = \boxed{0.9554}$$

**Takeaway.** The meter reads 4.5% low. To read closer to $\mathcal{E}$, you need $R \gg r$. Real digital voltmeters have about 10 MΩ, which makes $R/(r + R) \approx 0.999996$.

## Problem 3: A voltmeter loading a resistor
> A 0.95 MΩ voltmeter is placed in parallel with a 77.5 kΩ resistor. Find (a) the combined resistance, (b) the percent increase in current if the voltage stays the same, (c) the percent decrease in voltage if the current stays the same.

**What's happening.** The voltmeter is a second path in parallel with the resistor, so the combination has **less** resistance than the resistor alone. That's the "loading" error: the meter changes the thing it's measuring.

**(a) Parallel combination.** Parallel resistors share voltage and their currents add, $I = V/R_1 + V/R_2$, which gives $\frac{1}{R_p} = \frac1{R_1} + \frac1{R_2}$. For two:
$$R_p = \frac{R_1R_2}{R_1 + R_2} = \frac{(950\ \text{k}\Omega)(77.5\ \text{k}\Omega)}{950 + 77.5\ \text{k}\Omega} = \boxed{71.65\ \text{k}\Omega}$$
(Work in kΩ throughout: 0.95 MΩ = 950 kΩ.)

**(b) Same voltage, so how much more current?** Ohm's law with $V$ fixed: $I \propto 1/R$. So
$$\frac{I}{I_0} = \frac{V/R_p}{V/R} = \frac{R}{R_p} = \frac{77.5}{71.65} = 1.0816 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{8.16\%\ \text{increase}}$$
Shortcut: the extra current is exactly what the voltmeter draws, $V/R_{meter}$, so the fraction is $R/R_{meter} = 77.5/950 = 8.16\%$.

**(c) Same current, so how much less voltage?** Ohm's law with $I$ fixed: $V \propto R$. So
$$\frac{V_0 - V}{V_0} = 1 - \frac{R_p}{R} = 1 - \frac{71.65}{77.5} = \boxed{7.54\%\ \text{decrease}}$$

**Why (b) and (c) aren't the same number.** One is $R/R_p - 1$ and the other is $1 - R_p/R$. Percent up and percent down use different baselines, and they only match when the change is tiny (like Problem 4).

**Takeaway.** A voltmeter only about 12× bigger than the resistor causes a ~8% error. For good measurements the voltmeter needs to be **much** larger, around 100× or more.

## Problem 4: An ammeter loading a resistor
> A 0.0205 Ω ammeter is placed in series with a 9.5 Ω resistor. Find (a) the combined resistance, (b) the percent decrease in current if the voltage stays the same, (c) the percent increase in voltage if the current stays the same.

**What's happening.** The ammeter is in series, so its resistance **adds** and the current drops a little.

**(a) Series combination.** Series elements carry the same current and their voltages add, $V = IR_1 + IR_2$, so $R_s = R_1 + R_2$:
$$R_s = 9.5\ \Omega + 0.0205\ \Omega = \boxed{9.5205\ \Omega}$$
> [!note] About your answer
> Expert TA shows your entry as $9.4795\ \Omega$, which is $9.5 - 0.0205$. It was accepted because it's within about 0.4% of the right value, but series resistances **add**. The correct value is $9.5205\ \Omega$.

**(b) Same voltage, so how much less current?** $I \propto 1/R$:
$$\frac{I_0 - I}{I_0} = 1 - \frac{R}{R_s} = 1 - \frac{9.5}{9.5205} = \boxed{0.215\%}$$

**(c) Same current, so how much more voltage?** $V \propto R$:
$$\frac{\Delta V}{V_0} = \frac{R_s - R}{R} = \frac{0.0205}{9.5} = \boxed{0.216\%}$$

**Takeaway.** The ammeter's resistance is only 0.2% of the circuit's, so the error is only about 0.2%. Here (b) and (c) are nearly equal because the change is tiny (compare with Problem 3).

# HW 10.5: RC circuits

## The one idea behind every RC problem
A capacitor can't charge or discharge instantly, because charge has to flow through a resistor, and the resistor limits the current. The rate of flow is proportional to how far the capacitor is from its final state, and **that** is what makes everything exponential.

**Where the formulas come from (discharging).** Loop rule around a capacitor discharging through $R$: $\frac{q}{C} - iR = 0$, with $i = -\frac{dq}{dt}$ (the charge is decreasing). So
$$\frac{dq}{dt} = -\frac{q}{RC} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q(t) = Q_0e^{-t/RC}$$
The combination $\tau = RC$ has units of seconds ($\Omega\cdot\text{F} = \frac{\text{V}}{\text{A}}\cdot\frac{\text{C}}{\text{V}} = \frac{\text{C}}{\text{A}} = \text{s}$) and sets the speed.

| | Charging (from 0) | Discharging (from $Q_0$) |
|---|---|---|
| Charge | $q = C\mathcal{E}(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$ | $q = Q_0e^{-t/\tau}$ |
| Capacitor voltage | $V_C = \mathcal{E}(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$ | $V_C = V_0e^{-t/\tau}$ |
| Current | $i = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R}e^{-t/\tau}$ | $i = \frac{V_0}{R}e^{-t/\tau}$ |

**"Time to reach a fraction" trick.** If something decays to a fraction $f$ of its start, $e^{-t/\tau} = f$, so
$$t = \tau\ln\frac{1}{f} \qquad (\text{half: } t = \tau\ln 2 \approx 0.693\,\tau)$$

## Problem 1: Two capacitors in series discharging through a resistor
> Capacitors $3C$ and $5C$ ($C = 0.085$ F) are in series with $R = 5.5\ \Omega$ (and a switch). (a) How long until the charge drops by 75%? (b) If it was charged by a 10.0 V source, how much charge did the capacitors have to begin with?

```tikz
\usepackage{circuitikz}
\begin{document}
\begin{circuitikz}
\draw (0,2) to[C,l=$3C$] (2,2) to[C,l=$5C$] (4,2) to[R,l=$R$] (4,0) to[nos,l=$S$] (0,0) -- (0,2);
\end{circuitikz}
\end{document}
```

**Step 1: Reduce to one capacitor.** The two capacitors are in **series**, so they carry the **same charge** and their voltages add: $V = \frac{Q}{C_1} + \frac{Q}{C_2}$, which gives $\frac{1}{C_{eq}} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2}$. (Opposite of resistors.)
$$C_{eq} = \frac{(3C)(5C)}{3C + 5C} = \frac{15}{8}C = 1.875(0.085\ \text{F}) = 0.1594\ \text{F}$$

**Step 2: Time constant.**
$$\tau = RC_{eq} = (5.5\ \Omega)(0.1594\ \text{F}) = 0.8766\ \text{s}$$

**(a) "Drop by 75%."** This is the classic trap: dropping **by** 75% means **25% is left**, so $f = 0.25$, not 0.75.
$$e^{-t/\tau} = 0.25 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t = \tau\ln\frac{1}{0.25} = \tau\ln 4 = (0.8766)(1.3863) = \boxed{1.215\ \text{s}}$$
Check: one half-life ($0.693\tau = 0.61$ s) leaves 50% and two leave 25%, so $t = 2 \times 0.6076 = 1.215$ s ✓.

**(b) Initial charge.** Fully charged by 10.0 V, the series pair acts like one capacitor $C_{eq}$ with 10 V across it:
$$Q_0 = C_{eq}V = (0.1594\ \text{F})(10.0\ \text{V}) = \boxed{1.59\ \text{C}}$$
Why not add two charges? In series, each capacitor holds the **same** $Q = 1.59$ C: the battery moves 1.59 C onto the outer plates, and the inner plates just separate charge between them. Check: $V_{3C} = \frac{1.59}{0.255} = 6.25$ V and $V_{5C} = \frac{1.59}{0.425} = 3.75$ V, which add to 10 V ✓.
> [!tip] If 1.59 is marked wrong
> The wording "total charge both capacitors" is ambiguous. Expert TA's intended answer is almost certainly $C_{eq}V = 1.59$ C. If it's rejected, the other reading is $2Q = 3.19$ C.

## Problem 2: Charging an RC circuit
> An uncharged $C = 52\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor and $R = 65\ \Omega$ are in series with a $\mathcal{E} = 5.5$ V battery. (a) Express $\tau$. (b) Its value in μs. (c) Express the maximum charge $Q$. (d) Its value in μC.

**(a)** $\boxed{\tau = RC}$, from the differential equation above (the same $RC$ appears for charging).

**(b)**
$$\tau = (65\ \Omega)(52\times10^{-6}\ \text{F}) = 3.38\times10^{-3}\ \text{s} = \boxed{3380\ \mu\text{s}}$$
Unit trick: Ω × μF = μs, so $65 \times 52 = 3380$ μs directly.

**(c)** $\boxed{Q = C\epsilon}$. Why: when fully charged, no current flows ($i \to 0$), so there's no drop across $R$, and the whole battery voltage sits across the capacitor. Then $Q = CV = C\epsilon$.

**(d)**
$$Q = (52\ \mu\text{F})(5.5\ \text{V}) = \boxed{286\ \mu\text{C}}$$
Unit trick: μF × V = μC.

## Problem 3: The lab capacitor and the voltmeter
> $\varepsilon = 4.5$ V supply, $C = 330\ \mu\text{F}$. At switch position **a** the capacitor charges from the supply. At **b** it's disconnected from the supply and connected to a voltmeter with $R = 10.0\ \text{M}\Omega$.

**(a) What happens to the charge when the switch goes to a?**
**Answer: "It will quickly (but not instantaneously) increase to a maximum value."**

Reasoning, one choice at a time:
- **Not instantaneous:** charge must flow through *some* resistance (wires, the supply's internal resistance), so $\tau > 0$.
- **Quickly:** that resistance is tiny, so $\tau = RC$ is tiny.
- **To a maximum value:** charging stops when $V_C = \varepsilon$, at $Q = C\varepsilon$. It doesn't keep increasing "until disconnected." It levels off long before then.

**(b) Full charge.**
$$Q = C\varepsilon = (330\times10^{-6}\ \text{F})(4.5\ \text{V}) = \boxed{1.485\times10^{-3}\ \text{C}}$$

**(c) Time for the voltmeter to read half.** At b, the capacitor discharges **through the voltmeter**, so the voltmeter's internal resistance is the $R$ in $\tau$. The voltmeter reads $V_C$, which decays as $V_0e^{-t/\tau}$.
$$\tau = RC = (10.0\times10^6\ \Omega)(330\times10^{-6}\ \text{F}) = 3300\ \text{s}$$
$$\frac{V}{V_0} = \frac12 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_{1/2} = \tau\ln 2 = (3300)(0.6931) = \boxed{2287\ \text{s}}\ (\approx 38\ \text{min})$$

**(d) It actually took only 1000 s. Which explanations are possible?**
A faster decay means a **smaller** $\tau = RC$. The observed time gives $\tau = \frac{1000}{\ln 2} = 1443$ s, less than half the expected 3300 s. So either $R$ or $C$ is smaller than labeled. Go through the choices:

| Choice | Effect | Possible? |
|---|---|---|
| Voltmeter's resistance significantly lower than 10 MΩ | smaller $R$, smaller $\tau$ (it would need about 4.4 MΩ) | ✅ **Yes** |
| Power supply's internal resistance in series with the capacitor | the supply is **disconnected** at b, so it can't matter | ❌ |
| Capacitor damaged, plates closer together | $C = \varepsilon A/d$: smaller $d$ means **larger** $C$, which would be slower | ❌ |
| Dielectric degraded, decreasing the capacitance | smaller $C$, smaller $\tau$ (it would need about 144 μF) | ✅ **Yes** |
| Power supply's internal resistance in parallel with the capacitor | again, the supply isn't in the circuit at b | ❌ |

**Answer: select the 1st and 4th** (voltmeter resistance lower; dielectric degraded, decreasing $C$).

## Problem 4: Windshield wiper timer
> A $0.500\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor and a variable resistor set the wiper's timing. What range of $R$ gives time constants from 2.00 s to 15.0 s?

**Why $R = \tau/C$.** The timer's delay **is** the time constant, $\tau = RC$. $C$ is fixed, so $R$ is the knob: $R = \tau/C$. A bigger $R$ means a slower charge and a longer pause between wipes.

**(a) Minimum** (shortest delay, 2.00 s):
$$R_{min} = \frac{2.00\ \text{s}}{0.500\times10^{-6}\ \text{F}} = 4.00\times10^6\ \Omega = \boxed{4.00\ \text{M}\Omega}$$

**(b) Maximum** (longest delay, 15.0 s):
$$R_{max} = \frac{15.0\ \text{s}}{0.500\times10^{-6}\ \text{F}} = 3.00\times10^7\ \Omega = \boxed{30.0\ \text{M}\Omega}$$

Unit trick: s ÷ μF = MΩ, so it's just $2/0.5 = 4$ and $15/0.5 = 30$.
