## The one idea behind this whole set: Ampère's law
$$\oint \vec B\cdot d\vec\ell = \mu_0 I_{enc}$$
Walk around a closed loop, add up $B$ along the path, and you get $\mu_0$ times the current poking through the loop. Two things decide the answer:
1. **Which currents are inside the loop.** Currents outside contribute nothing to the integral (their field goes up on one side and down on the other and cancels).
2. **The sign.** Curl the fingers of your right hand along the loop's direction; your thumb points in the **positive** current direction.
   - Loop goes **counterclockwise** (on the page) → thumb **out of the page** → ⊙ currents count $+$, ⊗ count $-$.
   - Loop goes **clockwise** → thumb **into the page** → ⊗ currents count $+$, ⊙ count $-$.

## Problem 1: Ranking four Amperian loops
![[HW 12.2 P1 Amperian loops.png|400]]

> Rank $\oint_A$, $\oint_B$, $\oint_C$, $\oint_D$ of $\vec B\cdot d\vec\ell$ from most negative to most positive.

The currents: $I$ ⊙ at the top, $2I$ ⊙ on the left, two $I$ ⊗ in the middle, one $I$ ⊗ on the right.

**Loop A (black, tall).**
- Direction: arrow at the top points right, left side goes up → **clockwise** → into the page is positive.
- Inside: top $I$ ⊙, both middle $I$ ⊗. The $2I$ is to the left of it; the right $I$ is outside.
- $I_{enc} = -I + I + I = +I$
- $\oint_A = +\mu_0 I$

**Loop B (green, skinny).**
- Direction: arrow at the top points left, right side goes up → **counterclockwise** → out of the page is positive.
- Inside: the same three currents as A (top $I$ ⊙, both middle $I$ ⊗).
- $I_{enc} = +I - I - I = -I$
- $\oint_B = -\mu_0 I$

Same currents as A, opposite direction, so opposite sign. That's the whole point of including both.

**Loop C (blue, left).**
- Direction: top arrow points right → clockwise (it doesn't end up mattering).
- Inside: $2I$ ⊙ and both middle $I$ ⊗. The top $I$ ⊙ is just *above* the blue loop, so it's outside.
- $I_{enc} = -2I + I + I = 0$
- $\oint_C = 0$

$\oint \vec B \cdot d\vec\ell = 0$ doesn't mean $B = 0$ on the loop. It just means the positive and negative contributions around the path cancel.

**Loop D (red, right).**
- Direction: top arrow points right, left side goes up → **clockwise** → into the page is positive.
- Inside: both middle $I$ ⊗ and the right $I$ ⊗.
- $I_{enc} = I + I + I = +3I$
- $\oint_D = +3\mu_0 I$

**Ranking:**
$$\underbrace{\oint_B}_{-\mu_0 I} < \underbrace{\oint_C}_{0} < \underbrace{\oint_A}_{+\mu_0 I} < \underbrace{\oint_D}_{+3\mu_0 I}$$
Drag in the order **B, C, A, D**.

## Problem 2: Straight wire touching a current loop
![[HW 12.2 P2 wire and loop.png|400]]

### Part (a): Which way must $I_2$ flow to cancel the loop's field at the center?

**Step 1: field from the loop.** The arrow on the right side of the loop points up, so $I_1$ runs **counterclockwise**. Curl your right fingers counterclockwise; your thumb points **out of the page**. So the loop makes $\vec B$ out of the page at its center.

**Step 2: what the wire needs to do.** To oppose that, the wire's field at the center must point **into the page**.

**Step 3: right-hand rule for a straight wire.** Thumb along the current, fingers curl around the wire. The center is **above** the wire. Try "current to the left": thumb pointing left, your fingers come over the top of the wire going *into* the page. ✓

$$\boxed{I_2 \text{ flows to the Left}}$$

**Check with the cross product:** $\vec B \propto \hat I \times \hat r$, where $\hat r$ points from the wire to the point. $(-\hat x)\times(+\hat y) = -\hat z$ = into the page ✓.

### Part (b) (not in your screenshot)
The usual follow-up is "what ratio $I_2/I_1$ makes the field at the center zero?" The center is a distance $R$ from the wire (the wire touches the loop), so set the two field magnitudes equal:
$$\underbrace{\frac{\mu_0 I_1}{2R}}_{\text{loop, at center}} = \underbrace{\frac{\mu_0 I_2}{2\pi R}}_{\text{long wire, distance } R}$$
$$\frac{I_2}{I_1} = \pi \approx 3.14$$
The wire needs about 3× the current because a straight wire's field is weaker at distance $R$ than a loop's field at its own center (all of the loop is a distance $R$ away and every piece adds in the same direction). If your part (b) asks something different, send it over.

## Problem 3: Hollow conducting cylinder
![[HW 12.2 P3 hollow cylinder.png|300]]

> $r_1 = 5.11$ cm, $r_2 = 9.52$ cm, $I = 44.8$ A spread uniformly over the shaded area (into the page). Find $B$ at 1.33 cm, 6.51 cm, and 12.63 cm.

**Setup for all three parts.** The cylinder is symmetric, so $B$ has the same magnitude everywhere on a circle of radius $r$ around the axis, and points along that circle. Use a circular Amperian loop of radius $r$:
$$B(2\pi r) = \mu_0 I_{enc} \quad\Rightarrow\quad B = \frac{\mu_0 I_{enc}}{2\pi r}$$
The only work in each part is figuring out $I_{enc}$.

### Part (a): $r = 1.33$ cm (inside the hole)
$1.33 < 5.11$, so the loop sits in the empty hole. No current passes through it: $I_{enc} = 0$.
$$\boxed{B = 0\ \mu\text{T}}$$

### Part (b): $r = 6.51$ cm (inside the metal)
$5.11 < 6.51 < 9.52$, so the loop passes through the conductor and only encloses *part* of the current. The current is uniform over the ring's area, so the enclosed fraction = enclosed area / total area. The area of a ring is $\pi(r_{out}^2 - r_{in}^2)$, and the $\pi$'s cancel:
$$I_{enc} = I\,\frac{r^2 - r_1^2}{r_2^2 - r_1^2} = 44.8\ \text{A}\times\frac{(6.51)^2 - (5.11)^2}{(9.52)^2 - (5.11)^2} = 44.8\times\frac{42.38 - 26.11}{90.63 - 26.11} = 44.8\times 0.2522 = 11.30\ \text{A}$$
(cm² cancel in the ratio, so no unit conversion needed there.)
$$B = \frac{\mu_0 I_{enc}}{2\pi r} = \frac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(11.30)}{2\pi(0.0651\ \text{m})} = \frac{(2\times10^{-7})(11.30)}{0.0651} = 3.47\times10^{-5}\ \text{T}$$
$$\boxed{B = 34.7\ \mu\text{T}}$$

### Part (c): $r = 12.63$ cm (outside)
$12.63 > 9.52$, so the loop encloses all of the current: $I_{enc} = 44.8$ A. From outside, the cylinder looks exactly like a thin wire.
$$B = \frac{(2\times10^{-7})(44.8)}{0.1263} = 7.09\times10^{-5}\ \text{T}$$
$$\boxed{B = 70.9\ \mu\text{T}}$$

**Sanity check.** $B$ starts at 0 in the hole, grows through the metal as more current gets enclosed, peaks at the outer surface, then falls off as $1/r$. At the outer surface $B = (2\times10^{-7})(44.8)/0.0952 = 94\ \mu$T; part (b) is below that and part (c) is below that ✓.

**Shortcut:** $\dfrac{\mu_0}{2\pi} = 2\times10^{-7}$ T·m/A. Use it every time you see $\mu_0/(2\pi r)$.

## Problem 4: Number of turns in a solenoid
> $L = 78.9$ cm, $B = 0.352$ T at the center, $I = 2.04$ A. Find $N$.

**Why this formula.** Inside a long solenoid the field is uniform and (from Ampère's law with a rectangular loop) equals
$$B = \mu_0 n I, \qquad n = \frac{N}{L}\ \text{(turns per meter)}$$

**Solve for $N$.**
$$N = \frac{BL}{\mu_0 I} = \frac{(0.352\ \text{T})(0.789\ \text{m})}{(4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{T·m/A})(2.04\ \text{A})} = \frac{0.2777}{2.564\times10^{-6}}$$
$$\boxed{N \approx 1.08\times10^{5}\ \text{turns}}\quad(108{,}300)$$

**Sanity check.** 0.35 T is a *strong* field for 2 A, so you need a lot of turns: about 137,000 per meter. That's unrealistic for one layer of wire (it would need ~7 μm wire), so it would have to be many layers wound on top of each other. The number is just what the formula gives. Remember to convert cm → m; forgetting that gives 100× too many turns.

## Formulas used
| Situation | Field | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Any closed loop | $\oint \vec B\cdot d\vec\ell = \mu_0 I_{enc}$ | Ampère's law |
| Long straight wire | $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$ | Ampère, circular loop |
| Center of a circular loop | $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2R}$ | Biot–Savart |
| Inside a long solenoid | $B = \mu_0 n I = \mu_0 \dfrac{N}{L} I$ | Ampère, rectangular loop |
| Uniform current in a ring, $r_1<r<r_2$ | $I_{enc} = I\dfrac{r^2-r_1^2}{r_2^2-r_1^2}$ | area fraction |
